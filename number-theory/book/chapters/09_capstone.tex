\chapter{Capstone studio}
\label{chap:capstone}
\chaptertag{Choose a thread, extend it, and explain it}

The capstone is not a miniature research paper. It is a carefully explained mathematical journey: a motivating question, small examples, one substantial proof, and a clear account of how course ideas connect.

\section{Project menu}

Choose one project. Projects A--D develop familiar course threads;
Projects E--N introduce further applications, history, and theory.
Each of the new options has a required core and an optional extension.
The core should fit the four-to-six page exposition described below;
an extension is an opportunity to go further after completing it.
Preparation notes identify useful background, including material that
can be learned as part of the project. Programming is optional unless
you choose an extension that calls for it.

\subsection*{A. Rational approximation and Pell equations}
Choose a nonsquare $d$. Compute the continued fraction of $\sqrt d$, identify convergents that solve $x^2-dy^2=\pm1$, and prove at least one recurring pattern. Include a number-line or lattice illustration.

\subsection*{B. Symmetry, orbits, and divisibility}
Generalize necklace counting to length $n$. Classify possible orbit sizes for one composite $n$, explain stabilizers in concrete language, and derive a divisibility identity. Section~\ref{sec:mobius-inversion} supplies a formula for every length. As a bonus extension, choose another poset from Section~\ref{sec:poset-inversion} and explain what its inverse counts.

\subsection*{C. Pascal's triangle modulo a prime}
Visualize Pascal's triangle modulo $p$. Develop and prove a digit rule (Lucas's theorem is the natural destination), then explain how Kummer's carry rule and the visual pattern encode the same arithmetic.

\subsection*{D. Prime-counting laboratory}
Compare $\pi(x)$ with $x/\log x$, $\operatorname{Li}(x)$, and the Chebyshev bounds over a range your computer can handle. Explain which plots are evidence and which claims are proved. Reconstruct one link in Chebyshev's proof in full detail.

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{E. RSA: construction, correctness, and weaknesses}
\textbf{Preparation.} Modular inverses, Fermat's theorem, and the Chinese
remainder theorem (Chapter~\ref{chap:modular}).

\textbf{Core.} Choose distinct odd primes $p,q$, set $n=pq$, and choose
$1<e<\varphi(n)$ relatively prime to $\varphi(n)$. Explain how extended
Euclid finds a positive $d$ with $ed\congr1\pmod{\varphi(n)}$.
Describe encryption and decryption
and prove that
\[
  (m^e)^d\congr m\pmod n
\]
for \emph{every} message residue $m$, including those not relatively prime
to $n$. Give a complete numerical example. Explain how knowing the
factorization of $n$ gives a private exponent, and demonstrate a weak
choice of modulus, such as a product of nearby primes that can be found
by a difference-of-squares search. Distinguish the correctness proof
from an argument about the difficulty of recovering a message.

\textbf{Extension.} Implement the example using repeated squaring, or
explain why $\operatorname{lcm}(p-1,q-1)$ can replace $\varphi(n)$ when
choosing $d$.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/resources/mit18_781s12_lec6/}{MIT 18.781 notes on primality, factoring, and RSA}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{F. Fermat's Last Theorem: how the methods changed}
\textbf{Preparation.} Divisibility, Pythagorean triples, and proof by
infinite descent; an interest in mathematical history.

\textbf{Core.} Organize a historical account around a few decisive changes
of method, such as Fermat's descent, Kummer's work on factorization, and
the route through elliptic curves and modularity that led to Wiles's
proof. Explain the mathematical obstacle each development addressed.
Include a complete proof of the exponent-four case using infinite
descent. State clearly which later theorems you quote; the modern proof
should be explained through its main implications, with their precise
scope, while the elementary case supplies the fully worked proof.
Support historical claims with identifiable sources.

\textbf{Extension.} Analyze the two factorizations
$6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ in $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$.
Prove that the displayed factors are irreducible and explain why unique
factorization cannot simply be assumed in a larger number system.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://abelprize.no/abel-prize-laureates/2016}{The Abel Prize's
Wiles materials}, including its account of the exponent-four proof.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{G. Smith normal form and integer equations}
\textbf{Preparation.} The Euclidean algorithm, integer matrices, and
elementary row and column operations.

\textbf{Core.} Prove that an integer matrix $A$ can be transformed to a
diagonal matrix $D=UAV$, where $U,V$ are square integer matrices of
determinant $\pm1$. If $r$ is the rank of $A$, the $r$ nonzero diagonal
entries are positive and satisfy
\[
  d_1\divides d_2\divides\cdots\divides d_r.
\]
Explain why the reduction terminates and why each operation is reversible
over the integers. Work out a $3\times3$ example, keeping track of the
operations, and use the resulting form to decide when $Ax=b$ has an
integer solution and describe all its solutions.

\textbf{Extension.} Prove uniqueness of the diagonal entries using gcds
of minors, or explain how the form describes
$\Z^m/A\Z^n$ and leads to the classification of finitely generated
abelian groups.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-18-012-algebra-ii-student-notes-spring-2022/mit18_702s22_lect21.pdf}{MIT Algebra II, Lecture 21: Smith Normal Form}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{H. Shamir secret sharing}
\textbf{Preparation.} Finite-field arithmetic and Lagrange interpolation
(Section~\ref{sec:crt-interpolation}).

\textbf{Core.} Explain a $t$-out-of-$n$ sharing scheme, with
$2\le t\le n<p$ and $p$ prime. To share $s\in\F_p$, choose
$a_1,\ldots,a_{t-1}$ independently and uniformly from $\F_p$ and set
\[
  f(X)=s+a_1X+\cdots+a_{t-1}X^{t-1}.
\]
Participant $i$ receives $(i,f(i))$, for $1\le i\le n$.
Prove that any $t$ shares reconstruct $s$, and use a counting argument
to prove that the distribution of any fewer than $t$ shares is the same
for every possible secret. Work out a three-out-of-five example over
$\F_{17}$. Notice that the degree is \emph{at most} $t-1$: zero leading
coefficients must remain among the random choices.

\textbf{Extension.} Write a small sharing and reconstruction program,
or connect the scheme to the polynomial codes in Project I.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://65610.csail.mit.edu/2025/lec/l11-ss.pdf}{Yael Kalai's MIT
notes on secret sharing}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{I. Reed--Solomon error-correcting codes}
\textbf{Preparation.} Finite fields, polynomial root bounds, and
interpolation (Section~\ref{sec:crt-interpolation} and
Appendix~\ref{app:finite-fields}).

\textbf{Core.} Choose $n$ distinct points in $\F_q$, with
$1\le k\le n\le q$, and encode a polynomial of degree less than $k$
by its values at these points. Define the distance between two codewords
as the number of positions where they differ. Prove that the minimum
distance between distinct codewords is $n-k+1$, and deduce how many
erasures or errors can be recovered uniquely. Distinguish an erasure
(a known missing position) from an error (an incorrect value at an
unknown position). Give an example using five evaluation points over
$\F_7$ and degree at most two: recover two missing values, then work a
separate example with one incorrect value. Explain how you locate and
check that error.

\textbf{Extension.} Implement a decoder or explain the
Berlekamp--Welch decoding method.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~anuprao/pubs/codingtheory/lecture4.pdf}{Anup Rao's notes on Reed--Solomon codes}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{J. When Fermat's primality test lies}
\textbf{Preparation.} Fermat's theorem, CRT, and multiplicative orders;
cyclicity of $\F_p^\times$ is useful for the main criterion.

\textbf{Core.} Define a Fermat pseudoprime to a specified coprime base,
and define a Carmichael number as a composite $n>1$ for which
$a^{n-1}\congr1\pmod n$ for every $a$ relatively prime to $n$.
Prove that $561=3\cdot11\cdot17$ has this property. Prove Korselt's
criterion: a composite integer $n$ is Carmichael exactly when it is
squarefree and $p-1\divides n-1$ for every prime divisor $p$ of $n$.
Use the criterion to check further examples and explain why passing a
Fermat test does not certify primality.

\textbf{Extension.} Explain the Miller--Rabin test and exhibit a witness
that rejects $561$, or investigate how the criterion guides a search
for other Carmichael numbers.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/ugradnumthy/carmichaelkorselt.pdf}{Keith Conrad, \emph{Carmichael Numbers and Korselt's Criterion}}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{K. Gaussian integers and sums of two squares}
\textbf{Preparation.} Complex-number arithmetic, the Euclidean algorithm,
and unique factorization in the ordinary integers.

\textbf{Core.} Introduce $\Z[i]$ and the multiplicative norm
$N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2$. Prove Euclidean division by choosing a nearest
Gaussian integer to a quotient, and explain how the Euclidean algorithm
gives gcds and the factorization properties you use. Then prove that an
odd prime $p$ is a sum of two integer squares if and only if
$p\congr1\pmod4$. Include explicit divisions and factorizations that
show the method in action.

\textbf{Extension.} Derive the criterion for a general positive integer
to be a sum of two squares, or explain how different Gaussian
factorizations yield different representations of the same integer.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/ugradnumthy/Zinotes.pdf}{Keith Conrad, \emph{The Gaussian Integers}}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{L. Quadratic reciprocity}
\textbf{Preparation.} Congruences modulo primes and Euler's criterion.
This is an ambitious option centered on a substantial proof.

\textbf{Core.} Define the Legendre symbol and state quadratic reciprocity
for distinct odd primes. Present one complete proof, developing the
lemmas it needs; a route through Gauss's lemma and lattice-point counting
is a natural choice. Explain the roles of the supplementary laws for
$-1$ and $2$, and use reciprocity to decide several explicitly chosen
quadratic-residue questions. Make clear which results are proved in the
project and which established facts are being quoted.

\textbf{Extension.} Compare the main idea with a second proof, or explain
the Jacobi symbol and why a Jacobi symbol of $1$ does not always imply
that a number is a square modulo a composite modulus.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/resources/mit18_781s12_lec9/}{MIT 18.781 notes on quadratic residues and reciprocity}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{M. Hensel lifting: solving congruences one digit at a time}
\textbf{Preparation.} Congruences modulo prime powers, polynomial
arithmetic, and the formal derivative of a polynomial.

\textbf{Core.} Let $p$ be prime, $f\in\Z[X]$, and $a\in\Z$. Suppose
$f(a)\congr0\pmod p$ but $f'(a)\not\congr0\pmod p$.
Prove that for each $k\ge1$ the chosen root $a$ has exactly one lift
modulo $p^k$. Derive the next-digit equation by substituting
$a_k+tp^k$ into $f$ and reducing modulo $p^{k+1}$.
Starting with $a=3$ modulo $7$, calculate successive solutions of
$x^2\congr2\pmod{7^k}$. Contrast this with $x^2\congr3\pmod3$,
whose root does not lift modulo $9$, and explain the failed hypothesis.

\textbf{Extension.} Interpret the compatible digits as a $p$-adic root,
or investigate an example in which a root with vanishing derivative
has more than one lift.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-781-theory-of-numbers-spring-2012/resources/mit18_781s12_lec6/}{MIT 18.781 notes on Hensel's lemma}.

\end{capstoneproject}

\begin{capstoneproject}
\subsection*{N. Farey fractions and Ford circles}
\textbf{Preparation.} Reduced fractions, B\'ezout's identity, and
elementary coordinate geometry. This is a particularly visual option.

\textbf{Core.} Define the Farey sequence of order $N$ as the reduced
fractions in $[0,1]$ with denominator at most $N$, listed in increasing
order. Prove that neighbors $a/b<c/d$ satisfy $bc-ad=1$, and explain
the role of their mediant $(a+c)/(b+d)$ as the order increases.
Associate to $a/b$ the circle with center $(a/b,1/(2b^2))$ and radius
$1/(2b^2)$. Prove that two distinct such circles are tangent exactly
when the corresponding fractions satisfy the determinant condition
$\abs{ad-bc}=1$. Draw and explain an example that displays both the
arithmetic and the geometry.

\textbf{Extension.} Connect the construction to continued fractions,
or prove that the Stern--Brocot tree contains every positive rational
number exactly once.

\textbf{Starting point.}
\href{https://www.maths.ed.ac.uk/~v1ranick/papers/ford.pdf}{L. R. Ford,
``Fractions,'' \emph{American Mathematical Monthly} \textbf{45}
(1938), 586--601}, especially its opening sections.

\end{capstoneproject}

\section{Required deliverables}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A four-to-six page mathematical exposition.
  \item At least one original diagram or data visualization.
  \item One theorem stated precisely and proved completely.
  \item A one-page ``proof map'' naming the definitions and earlier results used.
  \item A five-minute oral explanation, followed by questions.
\end{enumerate}

Include a worked example of your own and references with enough detail
to locate the arguments you used. A historical project has the same
complete-proof requirement; a computational project must explain why
its method works. Define unfamiliar objects, identify the main theorem
early, and complete the core before adding an extension. The references
above are starting points; reading an entire source is not required.

\begin{checkpoint}
Before submitting, underline every sentence that makes a mathematical claim. For each underline, ask: Is it a definition, an observed example, a cited theorem, or a claim I have proved? Ambiguous status is often the real source of unclear exposition.
\end{checkpoint}

\section{A final synthesis prompt}

In 500--700 words, respond to this statement:

\begin{quote}
Number theory repeatedly converts multiplication into something easier to organize: remainders, exponent vectors, additive logarithms, or equal-sized orbits.
\end{quote}

Use one precise example from three different chapters. Your response should include at least one equation and explain not only what the conversion does, but why it helps a proof.
