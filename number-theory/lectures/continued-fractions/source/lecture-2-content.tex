\teachcover{Why quadratic surds\\have repeating continued fractions}{1}{
First: the converse of Lagrange's theorem.

Then: when the repetition starts at the first digit.
}{
Begin with the question left open by Lecture 1. We know how a
repeating continued fraction produces a quadratic equation.
Today we start with a quadratic surd and prove that its continued
fraction must eventually repeat. Establish that result before
introducing Galois's additional criterion.
The proof uses rationalization, convergence of convergents,
and the pigeonhole principle. Source: book 3.8--3.9.
}

\teachslide{The converse we still need}{1}{
\textbf{Lecture 1 proved}
\[
 \text{eventually periodic CF}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \text{quadratic irrational}.
\]
\textbf{Today we prove}
\[
 \key{\text{quadratic irrational}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \text{eventually periodic CF}.}
\]
Every quadratic surd has an expansion of the form
\[
 [a_0;\ldots,a_{r-1},\overline{b_0,\ldots,b_{\ell-1}}].
\]
The finite prefix is allowed to be empty.
}{
The two implications together are usually called Lagrange's
theorem. Here \emph{the converse} means the direction left for
this lecture. A quadratic surd is an irrational real root of an
integer quadratic; we will spell out its square-root form shortly.
An overline means that the indicated block repeats forever.
The theorem makes no claim yet about where the repetition begins.
All continued fractions here are simple; rational finite expansions
are not called eventually periodic. Source: book Theorem 3.13.
}

\teachslide{The algorithm and its remaining tails}{2}{
Start with an irrational $x_0=x$ and repeat
\[
 a_n=\floor{x_n},\qquad
 \key{x_{n+1}=\frac1{x_n-a_n}}.
\]
Since $0<x_n-a_n<1$, we have $x_{n+1}>1$.
\[
 x_n=a_n+\frac1{x_{n+1}}
     =[a_n;a_{n+1},\ldots].
\]
The \key{complete quotient} $x_n$ is the remaining tail.

For $n\ge1$, $a_n\ge1$; the first digit $a_0$ can be any integer.
}{
Explain why the strict fractional-part inequalities continue to
hold. If a later complete quotient were rational, then
$x_n=a_n+1/x_{n+1}$ would make every preceding one rational.
Thus the algorithm never terminates for our irrational input.
Rebuild the index convention: $x_0$ is the original number,
$a_0$ its integer part, and $x_1$ the first remaining tail.
Source: book 3.5 and 3.8.
}

\teachslide{First see repetition happen: $\sqrt3$}{2}{
Here $x_0=\sqrt3$ and $a_0=1$. Rationalizing gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 x_1&=\frac1{\sqrt3-1}=\frac{\sqrt3+1}{2},
       &a_1&=1,\\[5pt]
 x_2&=\frac1{(\sqrt3+1)/2-1}=\sqrt3+1,
       &a_2&=2,\\[5pt]
 x_3&=\frac1{\sqrt3+1-2}=\frac{\sqrt3+1}{2}=x_1.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The same tail gives the same next digit and the same next tail.
\[
 \key{\sqrt3=[1;\overline{1,2}].}
\]
}{
Lecture 1 started from repeating digits and identified their value.
Here the direction is reversed: start with $\sqrt3$ and run the
algorithm until an exact tail returns. Use $1<\sqrt3<2$ to check
the two later integer parts. In the middle line,
$2/(\sqrt3-1)=\sqrt3+1$.
Stress that the certificate is equality of whole tails, not merely
the occurrence of the same digit twice. Source: book Example 3.16.
}

\teachslide{What will force a tail to return?}{1}{
We will write every tail using the same square root:
\[
 \key{x_n=\frac{P_n+\sqrt D}{Q_n}},
 \qquad P_n,Q_n\in\Z.
\]
\spacer
\textbf{1.} Find an exact rule for the two integers.

\textbf{2.} Prove that, eventually, both integers are bounded.

\textbf{3.} Apply the pigeonhole principle: a pair must repeat.
\spacer
The conjugate will supply the bounds in Step 2.
}{
Give the proof's purpose before introducing its algebra.
Keeping the same square root is not enough: there are infinitely
many integer pairs. We need both integrality and bounds.
The word \emph{state} below will mean just the pair $(P_n,Q_n)$.
The next slides establish the elementary facts about conjugates
that this proof needs, including why a conjugate follows the same
rational formulas. Source: book 3.8.
}

\teachslide{A surd and its other root}{2}{
Write $x=u+v\sqrt D$, where $u,v\in\Q$, $v\ne0$, and
$D$ is a positive nonsquare integer.

The factorization
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &(t-(u+v\sqrt D))(t-(u-v\sqrt D))\\
 &\hspace{25pt}=(t-u)^2-v^2D
               =t^2-2ut+u^2-v^2D
 \end{aligned}
\]
has rational coefficients and two distinct roots.

Thus the other root, called the \key{conjugate}, is
\[
 \key{x'=u-v\sqrt D.}
\]
}{
This proves the connection between the square-root expression and
the other root; it is not just a rule to memorize. Clear denominators
in the displayed polynomial to get integer coefficients.
Conversely, the quadratic formula puts every real quadratic
irrational in this form. Its discriminant is positive and nonsquare.
The representation in a fixed square root is unique: equality of
two different rational coefficients of $\sqrt D$ would make
$\sqrt D$ rational. Any rational quadratic having $x$ as a root
is a scalar multiple of this one: cancel their quadratic terms,
and a remaining nonzero linear polynomial could not vanish at
the irrational $x$. Thus the other root is unambiguous.
The prime is not a derivative. Source: book 3.7.
}

\teachslide{Why conjugation respects arithmetic}{2}{
Let $z=u+v\sqrt D$ and $w=r+s\sqrt D$, with rational coefficients.
\[
 (z+w)'=(u+r)-(v+s)\sqrt D=z'+w'.
\]
For multiplication, expand both products:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 zw&=(ur+vsD)+(us+vr)\sqrt D,\\
 z'w'&=(ur+vsD)-(us+vr)\sqrt D=(zw)'.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Rational numbers stay fixed: \key{$c'=c$ for $c\in\Q$}.
\spacer
In particular, $(z-a)'=z'-a$ for an integer $a$.
}{
Extend the sign-change operation to all numbers in the same
square-root field, including rational ones with square-root
coefficient zero. No field terminology is needed on the slide.
The multiplication identity works because $(\sqrt D)^2=D$ and
$(-\sqrt D)^2=D$; the rational term is unchanged and the
square-root term changes sign. This is the explicit justification
for the arithmetic assertion at the end of Lecture 1.
It concerns algebraic conjugation of numbers; the matrix operation
$MNM^{-1}$ from Lecture 1 is a different use of the word.
Source: book 3.7, with the arithmetic expanded.
}

\teachslide{Why conjugation respects reciprocals}{2}{
For $z=u+v\sqrt D\ne0$, rationalize:
\[
 \frac1z=\frac{u-v\sqrt D}{u^2-v^2D}.
\]
Since $\sqrt D$ is irrational, $z\ne0$ also gives $z'\ne0$.
Thus $zz'=u^2-v^2D$ is a nonzero rational number.

Now change the sign of the square root:
\[
 \key{\left(\frac1z\right)'
       =\frac{u+v\sqrt D}{u^2-v^2D}
       =\frac1{z'}.}
\]
For $a,b,c,d\in\Q$ and $cz+d\ne0$, this gives
\[
 \left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)'
       =\frac{az'+b}{cz'+d}.
\]
}{
Justify nonvanishing, rather than silently dividing.
If $z'=u-v\sqrt D=0$ and $v\ne0$, then $\sqrt D=u/v$
would be rational. If $v=0$, then $z'=u=z\ne0$.
Thus $z'\ne0$ whenever $z\ne0$, and $zz'$ is nonzero.
The last line assumes rational $a,b,c,d$ and a nonzero
denominator. Its conjugate denominator is nonzero by the
same argument. These two arithmetic slides prove, in particular,
$(T_M(x))'=T_M(x')$ for Lecture 1's rational matrices.
Source: book 3.7--3.8, with the rationalization expanded.
}

\teachslide{Step 1: start with an integer pair}{2}{
Take $Ax^2+Bx+C=0$ with $A,B,C\in\Z$, $A\ne0$.

Choose the common sign of $A,B,C$ so that
\[
 x=\frac{-B+\sqrt D}{2A},\qquad D=B^2-4AC.
\]
Then
\[
 \key{P_0=-B,\quad Q_0=2A,\quad
 x=\frac{P_0+\sqrt D}{Q_0}.}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 D-P_0^2=-4AC=Q_0(-2C),\qquad
 \key{Q_0\mid D-P_0^2.}
\]
$D$ is positive and nonsquare; $Q_0$ may be negative.
}{
This shows existence of the integer representation, including
the divisibility condition that will make the recurrence integral.
If the desired root initially has a minus sign before the square
root, replace $(A,B,C)$ by $(-A,-B,-C)$:
$(B+\sqrt D)/(-2A)=(-B-\sqrt D)/(2A)$.
The discriminant does not change. Since $x$ is a real irrational,
$D>0$ and $D$ cannot be a square.
There is no need to make $D$ squarefree. For special examples we
can choose a smaller $D$ directly: $\sqrt3=(0+\sqrt3)/1$ has
the required divisibility. Source: book 3.8, Step 2.
}

\teachslide{Derive the rule by rationalizing}{3}{
Suppose $x_n=(P_n+\sqrt D)/Q_n$ and $a_n=\floor{x_n}$.
Set
\[
 \key{P_{n+1}=a_nQ_n-P_n}.
\]
Then
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 x_{n+1}
 &=\frac{Q_n}{\sqrt D-P_{n+1}}\\[4pt]
 &=\frac{Q_n(\sqrt D+P_{n+1})}{D-P_{n+1}^2}
  =\frac{P_{n+1}+\sqrt D}{Q_{n+1}},
 \end{aligned}
\]
where
\[
 \key{Q_{n+1}=\frac{D-P_{n+1}^2}{Q_n}.}
\]
}{
Start by subtracting the digit explicitly:
$x_n-a_n=(P_n+\sqrt D-a_nQ_n)/Q_n
             =(\sqrt D-P_{n+1})/Q_n$.
Taking the reciprocal gives the first displayed line.
Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt D+P_{n+1}$
to get the second. This derives both formulas and explains the
sign in $P_{n+1}$.
The new denominator is not yet known to be an integer;
the next slide proves exactly that. The square root and $D$
stay fixed throughout. Source: book equation (3.4).
}

\teachslide{Why the new pair is still integral}{2}{
Assume $Q_n\mid D-P_n^2$. Since
\[
 P_{n+1}^2-P_n^2
       =a_nQ_n(a_nQ_n-2P_n),
\]
$Q_n$ also divides $D-P_{n+1}^2$. Therefore
\[
 \key{Q_{n+1}=\frac{D-P_{n+1}^2}{Q_n}\in\Z.}
\]
$Q_{n+1}\ne0$, because $D$ is not a square.

Finally,
\[
 D-P_{n+1}^2=Q_nQ_{n+1}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_{n+1}\mid D-P_{n+1}^2.
\]
The same hypothesis is ready for the next step.
}{
This is an induction with a base case on the initialization slide.
The recurrence makes $P_{n+1}$ an integer immediately.
To see the divisibility for $Q_{n+1}$, subtract
$P_{n+1}^2-P_n^2$ from $D-P_n^2$; both are multiples of $Q_n$.
The final equality establishes the next induction hypothesis.
Divisibility is valid with negative denominators as well, so no
positivity assumption has slipped into Step 1.
Source: book 3.8, Step 2.
}

\teachslide{Step 2: can these integers keep growing?}{1}{
We now have, for every $n$,
\[
 x_n=\frac{P_n+\sqrt D}{Q_n},
 \qquad P_n,Q_n\in\Z,\quad Q_n\ne0.
\]
There are still infinitely many possible integer pairs.
\spacer
Track the other root as well:
\[
 \key{x_n'=\frac{P_n-\sqrt D}{Q_n}.}
\]
We will first locate $x_n'$, then use $x_n$ and $x_n'$
together to bound $P_n$ and $Q_n$.
}{
Pause at the gap in the argument. The same square root has
persisted, but that fact alone does not force any repetition.
The new ingredient is an inequality for the conjugate.
Avoid introducing Galois's theorem or the name \emph{reduced} here:
the inequalities are being proved as tools for Lagrange's converse.
Source: book 3.8.
}

\teachslide{The conjugate follows the same algebraic step}{2}{
The arithmetic we proved gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 x_{n+1}'&=\left(\frac1{x_n-a_n}\right)'
           =\frac1{(x_n-a_n)'}\\[4pt]
          &=\key{\frac1{x_n'-a_n}}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The digit in both formulas is \key{$a_n=\floor{x_n}$}.

For $x_0=\sqrt3$, we use $a_0=1$, so
\[
 x_1'=\frac1{-\sqrt3-1}=\frac{1-\sqrt3}{2}.
\]
Here $\floor{-\sqrt3}=-2$: taking a floor does not commute with conjugation.
}{
Every equality on the first display follows from the previous
arithmetic slides: reciprocals commute with conjugation, and
the integer $a_n$ remains fixed.
This sequence of conjugates is usually not the ordinary
continued-fraction algorithm started at $x'$.
The example makes the difference concrete. Each $x_n'$ is
irrational and hence cannot equal the integer $a_n$, so every
displayed denominator is nonzero. Source: book 3.8, Step 1.
}

\teachslide{Express a tail using the original number}{2}{
Let $p_k/q_k=[a_0;\ldots,a_k]$. Lecture 1's prefix matrix gives,
for $n\ge2$,
\[
 x=\frac{p_{n-1}x_n+p_{n-2}}
          {q_{n-1}x_n+q_{n-2}}.
\]
Cross-multiply and collect the terms containing $x_n$:
\[
 (q_{n-1}x-p_{n-1})x_n=p_{n-2}-q_{n-2}x.
\]
Thus
\[
 \key{x_n=-\frac{q_{n-2}x-p_{n-2}}
                    {q_{n-1}x-p_{n-1}}.}
\]
}{
Recall the prefix matrix explicitly:
$D(a_0)\cdots D(a_{n-1})
 =\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p_{n-1}&p_{n-2}\\
                          q_{n-1}&q_{n-2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
It acts on the unprocessed tail $x_n$ to give $x$.
The displayed rearrangement derives the inverse-prefix identity
instead of citing it without calculation.
The denominator cannot vanish: $x$ is irrational whereas
$p_{n-1}/q_{n-1}$ is rational. For $n\ge2$, both relevant
$q$'s are positive. Source: book 3.2 and 3.8.
}

\teachslide{Why the conjugates eventually become negative}{3}{
Since $p_k/q_k\to x$ and $x'\ne x$, for all large $k$,
\[
 \left|\frac{p_k}{q_k}-x\right|<\frac{|x'-x|}{2}.
\]
Thus $x'-p_k/q_k$ has the same sign as $x'-x$.

Conjugate the tail formula and factor out the positive $q$'s:
\[
 x_n'=-\frac{q_{n-2}\bigl(x'-p_{n-2}/q_{n-2}\bigr)}
                 {q_{n-1}\bigl(x'-p_{n-1}/q_{n-1}\bigr)}.
\]
For large $n$, numerator and denominator have the same sign.
\[
 \key{x_n'<0\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }n.}
\]
}{
Spend time on the sign argument. The convergent lies within half
the distance from $x$ to $x'$, so it is on the same side of $x'$
as $x$. Equivalently,
$x'-p_k/q_k=(x'-x)+(x-p_k/q_k)$, and the second term has
magnitude less than half that of the nonzero first term.
The difference is therefore positive if $x'>x$ and negative if
$x'<x$. For large $n$ this applies at both indices $n-2,n-1$.
The two positive denominators $q_{n-2},q_{n-1}$ preserve the signs,
and the minus sign in front of the ratio makes $x_n'$ negative.
Only convergence has been used, with no periodicity assumption.
The argument covers $x<0$ and $x'>x$ as well.
Source: book 3.8, Step 1.
}

\teachslide{One more step gives a fixed interval}{1}{
Once $x_n'<0$ with $n\ge1$, we have $a_n\ge1$, so
\[
 x_n'-a_n<-1.
\]
Taking the reciprocal gives
\[
 \key{-1<x_{n+1}'=\frac1{x_n'-a_n}<0.}
\]
The same argument applies at every later step.
\spacer
Therefore all sufficiently late tails satisfy
\[
 \key{x_n>1,\qquad -1<x_n'<0.}
\]
}{
The interval follows from an elementary reciprocal inequality:
if $t<-1$, then $-1<1/t<0$.
Negativity was proved on the previous slide; this slide proves
entry into the smaller interval and that later conjugates stay
there. These are separate facts.
The restriction $n\ge1$ ensures $a_n\ge1$, even if the original
number has a negative integer part. Source: book 3.8, Step 1.
}

\teachslide{Bound the denominator first}{1}{
For all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
 x_n=\frac{P_n+\sqrt D}{Q_n}>1,
 \qquad -1<x_n'=\frac{P_n-\sqrt D}{Q_n}<0.
\]
Subtract the two expressions:
\[
 x_n-x_n'=\frac{2\sqrt D}{Q_n}>1.
\]
The numerator is positive, so first $Q_n>0$.

Multiplying by this positive denominator then gives
\[
 \key{0<Q_n<2\sqrt D.}
\]
}{
Keep the sign check visible. We are not assuming that the
initial denominator was positive: the eventual inequalities
force positivity here. Only after that check is it legitimate
to multiply an inequality by $Q_n$ without reversing its sign.
The upper bound depends solely on the fixed integer $D$.
Source: book 3.8, equation (3.5).
}

\teachslide{Now bound the numerator integer}{1}{
Use $Q_n>0$ in the conjugate inequalities:
\[
 -1<\frac{P_n-\sqrt D}{Q_n}<0
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 -Q_n<P_n-\sqrt D<0.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \sqrt D-Q_n<P_n<\sqrt D.
\]
Since $Q_n<2\sqrt D$,
\[
 P_n>\sqrt D-Q_n>-\sqrt D.
\]
Together,
\[
 \key{|P_n|<\sqrt D,\qquad 0<Q_n<2\sqrt D.}
\]
}{
This is the second half of the integer bounds. Every inequality
now appears on the slide: multiply by a positive $Q_n$, add
$\sqrt D$, and substitute the already proved upper bound on $Q_n$.
Students should see why both the original number and its conjugate
were useful. One bounded real expression alone would not have
bounded both integers. Source: book 3.8, equation (3.5).
}

\teachslide{Step 3: only finitely many pairs remain}{2}{
Fix $D$. Every sufficiently late pair lies in
\[
 \key{|P_n|<\sqrt D,\qquad 0<Q_n<2\sqrt D},
 \qquad P_n,Q_n\in\Z.
\]
Each integer has only finitely many choices.

For example, with $D=3$ the possible bounds are
\[
 P_n\in\{-1,0,1\},\qquad Q_n\in\{1,2,3\}.
\]
There are infinitely many tails but only finitely many such pairs.
\[
 \key{(P_i,Q_i)=(P_j,Q_j)\quad\text{for some }i<j.}
\]
}{
Invoke the pigeonhole principle explicitly. For $D=3$ the box
contains at most nine integer pairs; some do not satisfy all
the original conditions, which only makes the possible set smaller.
We do not need to enumerate the actual orbit.
For general $D$, there are at most
$(2\lfloor\sqrt D\rfloor+1)\lfloor2\sqrt D\rfloor$ pairs in
the box. Finiteness is all the proof needs.
The first repeated pair need not involve index zero.
Source: book 3.8, Step 3.
}

\teachslide{A repeated pair forces repeating digits}{1}{
Equal pairs give $x_i=x_j$. Hence
\[
 a_i=\floor{x_i}=\floor{x_j}=a_j,
 \qquad
 x_{i+1}=\frac1{x_i-a_i}=\frac1{x_j-a_j}=x_{j+1}.
\]
Repeat the argument:
\[
 \key{x_{i+k}=x_{j+k},\qquad a_{i+k}=a_{j+k}\quad(k\ge0).}
\]
With $\ell=j-i$, the expansion is
\[
 \key{x=[a_0;\ldots,a_{i-1},\overline{a_i,\ldots,a_{j-1}}].}
\]
This proves the converse of Lagrange's theorem.
}{
This is the precise link from pigeonhole to continued fractions.
The update rule is deterministic: equal complete quotients
produce equal digits and equal successors. Induction gives the
entire repeated future. The displayed period has positive length
$j-i$, although it need not be the shortest period.
The prefix is omitted if $i=0$.
At minute 34 the main existence theorem is fully proved, without
using Galois's theorem. Source: book 3.8.
}

 \qquad 2<\sqrt6<3.

 P_{n+1}=a_nQ_n-P_n,\qquad

Record $(P_n,Q_n,a_n)$ until an entire pair repeats.

Then write the continued fraction, marking where the period begins.

\teachslide{Lagrange's theorem is now complete}{1}{
\[
 \key{\text{eventually periodic CF}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{quadratic irrational}.}
\]
\textbf{Lecture 1:} a repeating tail gives a quadratic equation;
a finite prefix preserves quadratic irrationality.
\spacer
\textbf{Today:} integer pairs, eventual bounds, and a repeated tail.
\spacer
We have proved that repetition must occur.

The remaining question is when it starts at the first digit.
}{

Only now move to Galois's additional assertion.
If the class needs more time, use the final eleven minutes to

\teachslide{Now ask for pure periodicity}{2}{
A quadratic irrational $x$ is called \key{reduced} if
\[
 \key{x>1,\qquad -1<x'<0.}
\]
We proved that all sufficiently late tails have these inequalities,
and that the inequalities persist under the next step.
\spacer
\textbf{Galois's theorem}
\[
 \key{x\text{ is purely periodic}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\text{ is reduced}.}
\]
For example, $\sqrt3$ is not reduced, but $(1+\sqrt3)/2$ is.
}{
Define the name using the inequalities already established.
Galois adds an exact criterion for removing the initial segment;
Lagrange has already established existence of a period for every
quadratic surd. In the example, $-\sqrt3<-1$, whereas
$(1-\sqrt3)/2$ is between $-1$ and 0 and its partner exceeds 1.
We will first prove sufficiency, then necessity.
A reduced $x_0$ has $a_0\ge1$, so the invariance argument
applies from the very first step as well.
Source: book Theorem 3.17.
}

\teachslide{Finiteness alone permits an initial segment}{1}{
A function on the finite set $\{0,1\}$ can satisfy
\[
 F(0)=1,\qquad F(1)=1.
\]
Starting at $0$ gives
\[
 0,\ 1,\ 1,\ 1,\ldots
\]
Finiteness forces a repeated state, but the start need not return.
\spacer
To remove the initial segment for a reduced surd, we will show:

\key{A tail has at most one reduced predecessor.}
}{
This isolates the additional logical task. Knowing that reduced
tails stay reduced and lie in a finite set does not yet imply
pure periodicity. The example is a finite forward-invariant
system with a transient first state.
The next slide proves uniqueness of a reduced predecessor;
existence of an arbitrary predecessor is not needed for rewinding
the actual orbit. Source: book 3.9.
}

\teachslide{Recover the previous digit from the conjugate}{3}{
Suppose a reduced $u$ has digit $a=\floor u$ and next tail $z$:
\[
 z=\frac1{u-a},\qquad z'=\frac1{u'-a}.
\]
Solve the second equation for $-1/z'$:
\[
 -\frac1{z'}=a-u'.
\]
Since $-1<u'<0$, we have $0<-u'<1$, and therefore
\[
 a<-\frac1{z'}<a+1.
\]
Thus the digit and the predecessor are determined by $z$:
\[
 \key{a=\floor{-1/z'},\qquad u=a+\frac1z.}
\]
}{
Give the inequality its full justification: add the integer $a$
to $0<-u'<1$. The unique integer part of $-1/z'$ is then $a$.
Once $a$ is known, the original equation recovers $u$.
This proves at most one reduced predecessor, not uniqueness
among all possible numbers preceding the same tail.
Equal values of $z$ have equal conjugates by the well-defined
sign-change operation proved earlier.
For a numerical check using exact surds, let $z=(1+\sqrt3)/2$.
Then $-1/z'=1+\sqrt3$, so the previous digit is 2 and
$u=2+1/z=1+\sqrt3$. Source: book equation (3.6).
}

\teachslide{Rewind the repetition to the start}{1}{
If $x_0$ is reduced, every $x_n$ is reduced.

The finite-pair proof gives $x_i=x_j$ for some $i<j$.
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 x_i&=x_j\\
 &\Downarrow\quad\text{unique reduced predecessors}\\
 x_{i-1}&=x_{j-1}\\
 &\ \vdots\\
 \key{x_0}&\key{=x_{j-i}.}
 \end{aligned}
\]
The deterministic algorithm now repeats from its first digit.
}{
If $i=0$, we already have the desired conclusion. Otherwise
apply uniqueness at indices $i,j$, then $i-1,j-1$, and continue
for exactly $i$ steps. Each actual predecessor is reduced because
the starting point was reduced and the condition persists.
The index difference stays $j-i$ throughout.
Forward iteration from $x_0=x_{j-i}$ gives equality of all
corresponding digits, including $a_0$.
This proves the sufficient direction of Galois. Source: book 3.9.
}

\teachslide{A pure period must also be reduced}{1}{
Suppose $x=[\overline{a_0,\ldots,a_{\ell-1}}]$.

Lecture 1 proves that $x$ is quadratic.

Removing whole periods gives
\[
 x_{k\ell}=x_0=x\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots).
\]
We proved that every sufficiently late tail satisfies
\[
 x_n>1,\qquad -1<x_n'<0.
\]
Choose $k$ large enough. Since $x_{k\ell}=x$, these hold for $x$.
\spacer
\key{This completes both directions of Galois's theorem.}
}{
The convergence argument in Lagrange's proof supplies eventual
entry into the inequalities independently of Galois.
Pure periodicity repeatedly returns to the original value, so that
value must itself have the eventual inequalities.
This proves necessity without another matrix calculation.
The proof of Galois is complete at minute 47.
Source: book 3.9.
}

\teachslide{Exit question: what does each step prove?}{1}{
\textbf{Lagrange:} why did we need
\[
 \key{\text{integer pairs}\quad+\quad\text{bounds}?}
\]
\spacer
\textbf{Galois:} what extra fact let us turn
\[
 x_i=x_j\quad\text{into}\quad x_0=x_{j-i}?
\]
\spacer
Explain why $\sqrt3$ has a period even though its conjugate is below $-1$.
}{
Expected answers: bounded integer pairs form a finite set,
so some complete quotient and then the whole future repeat.
Bounds on real numbers alone do not give a finite set, and
integer pairs without bounds can escape forever.
Unique reduced predecessors let us rewind a repeated state to
index zero. The number $\sqrt3$ is a quadratic surd, so Lagrange
applies; its later tails become reduced even though it is not
reduced itself. Its expansion is eventually, not purely, periodic.
Stop here at 50 minutes. Suggested follow-up: compute the period
of $\sqrt{13}$ or use the optional reducedness exercise.

\backupslide{The conjugate reverses the period}{
If $x=[\overline{b_0,\ldots,b_{\ell-1}}]$, then
\[
 \key{-\frac1{x'}=[\overline{b_{\ell-1},\ldots,b_0}].}
\]
Each digit matrix is symmetric, so reversing a product
transposes it.
\[
 N=\mat ABC{D_0}:
 \quad Cx^2+(D_0-A)x-B=0.
\]
The conjugate satisfies this equation too. Substitute $x'=-1/z$:
\[
 Bz^2+(D_0-A)z-C=0,
\]
the fixed-point equation for $N^T$.
}{

therefore has one positive root and one negative root.

is positive too, so they are the same root.

\backupslide{The same matrix produces Pell solutions}{
Lecture 1 gave

 U=\mat2312,\qquad T_U(\sqrt3)=\sqrt3.

Ordinary matrix multiplication gives

 U\begin{pmatrix}p\\q\end{pmatrix}
   =\begin{pmatrix}2p+3q\\p+2q\end{pmatrix}.
\]
\[
 (2p+3q)^2-3(p+2q)^2=p^2-3q^2.
\]
Starting from $(1,0)$ produces

 (2,1),\ (7,4),\ (26,15),\ldots,
